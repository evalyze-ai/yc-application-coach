# YC interview: prep, mock interview, debrief

## The format (YC interview guide and apply page, checked October 2026)

- A 10-minute conversation over Zoom. All founders should be on the call. Expect 2 or 3 YC partners, who have read the application and have it open.
- No time for small talk or formal presentations. YC says they do two things: ask questions and look at what you've built.
- YC says interviews are designed so you don't need much preparation and advises against heavy rehearsal. Progress between applying and interviewing is the best way to get an edge.
- Interviews for a batch mostly happen by video in the weeks after the deadline (for Winter 2027, November and December). Decisions are typically made the same day, and everyone who interviews gets feedback.
- YC may verify numbers you state.

Founder accounts add: invites can come with only a day or a few days' notice; partners often spend the time on whatever worried them most about the application; follow-up calls and second interviews happen; acceptances usually arrive by phone, rejections by email with specific feedback; how the interview felt predicts very little. REPORTED (many founder accounts, consistent with each other).

## Prep checklist

1. "What are you building?" in two plain sentences, said the same way by every founder.
2. A one-page metrics sheet beside the camera (template in `assets/interview-cheat-sheet.md`). YC says you don't need to memorize every number, but know what drives each one.
3. The answer to the weakest-link question from the application audit.
4. Answers to "we've seen this before," "why won't [incumbent] just build this," and "how does this become very large."
5. What's changed since applying, with dates. Ship something before the call if possible.
6. A story from a real user this week. Call a customer the day before.
7. Who takes which kinds of question, loosely. Don't talk over each other or pile on to each other's answers.
8. The demo loaded and ready to screen-share. Hardware on camera.
9. Camera, mic and connection tested; a quiet room; backup internet if possible.

## Answering

- Answer the question, then stop. Let partners pull deeper. Most answers should take about 10 to 15 seconds.
- Lead with the fact, then one supporting detail.
- "I don't know, here's how we'd find out" beats bluffing.
- Treat interruptions as partners managing their time, not hostility.
- Don't be defensive. State a weakness plainly and what you're doing about it.
- If you disagree with a premise, clarify with facts, politely.
- Every founder should answer something.

## Question bank

Not a leaked list. These are the questions founders consistently report plus ones that follow from YC's own guidance. The first one is almost always some version of "What are you working on?"

**Product**
- What are you building? Who is it for?
- What does a user actually do with it, step by step?
- What's live today and what's manual or planned?
- Can you show us?
- What's technically hard about this?
- What did you build first, and why?

**Users and demand**
- Who exactly is the user, and who pays?
- How do you know people want this?
- How many users? How many active, and what counts as active?
- What's your retention?
- Who is your best customer and why?
- What has surprised you about how users behave?
- Why do hesitant users hold back?
- What do people use today instead?

**Growth and distribution**
- Where do new users come from?
- What's your growth rate, over what period?
- How will you get your next 100 customers?
- What does it cost to acquire one?

**Money and market**
- How do you make money? What do you charge?
- Who has paid you, and how much?
- What does it cost you to deliver?
- How big can this get? What's the path to a very large company?

**Competition and insight**
- Who are your competitors? Which one worries you most?
- Lots of teams have tried this. What's different now and about you?
- Why won't the incumbent just add this?
- What do you understand that they don't?

**Team**
- How did you meet? How long have you worked together?
- Who does what? Why is the CEO the CEO?
- Who wrote the code?
- Are you all full-time? If not, when?
- How do you resolve disagreements?
- Who's your next hire?

**Hard questions**
- Why haven't you made more progress?
- What's the biggest risk to this company?
- What will you do if we don't fund you?
- Would you consider working on a different idea?
- What's the biggest mistake you've made so far?
- What will be your biggest problem six months from now?
- What changed since you applied?

| How will you get customers? | 2 | Name the channel that works now |
| How do you make money? | 2 | Pricing and who has paid |

Partner habits founders and partners have described: Gustaf Alströmer has said his favorite question is what you've done on the product since you applied. Dalton Caldwell opens with what you're working on or what you've learned, and listens for research and real opinions. His questions depend on the company: launch questions for a product that isn't live, regulatory questions for a regulated business, differentiation for a crowded market. YC's own guide lists questions for launched companies: where new users come from, growth, retention, unit economics, what makes people try you, what holds hesitant ones back, what has surprised you about user behavior.

## What tipped decisions, in founder accounts

REPORTED (founder posts and interviews collected in the research guides; outcomes as the founders told them):

1. **Progress between applying and interviewing.** YC's own top advice. Codebuff fixed its core feature the night before its interview. CopyCat built an MVP and signed paying beta users in the three weeks between interviews.
2. **A convincing answer to "we've seen this before."** Michael Seibel asked Infracost what made them different from the many teams who'd tried cloud cost tools.
3. **Proof of demand when it was doubted.** When partners questioned demand, Knowlify cited 75,000 videos generated in its first month.
4. **The team over the idea.** Onu and CopyCat were both told the founders were strong and the idea wasn't, and both got in after pivoting.
5. **Conviction.** Asked what he'd do if YC said no, Hassan Syyid (hotglue, S21) said he'd already left university and would keep going.
6. **Not being defensive.** Former partners have called defensiveness and inflated claims (calling one conversation a sale) close to disqualifying. "I don't know" is fine.

Founders who felt they bombed got in (Infracost, Traceloop, Beam, GRU Space), and others who felt great were rejected. Dalton Caldwell's 2023 talk says the biggest mistake is treating the interview as adversarial: the people asking are likely to be your partners if you get in, and they're asking themselves whether they could work with you. He said partners dislike memorized speeches and founders who don't listen, and warned against coaches who teach founders to hide weak spots.

## Forty deeper questions, for finding holes (not for memorizing)

A diligence bank from one of the research guides, not a YC list. Use the ones that fit the company to find unclear thinking or missing evidence before the call, then go back to building. Some won't apply at the founder's stage; say so rather than inventing an answer.

**Customer need and adoption.** 1. What does the user do right before needing your product? 2. What useful result exists after they finish? 3. Which part of that works today? 4. What would they do if your product disappeared? 5. Who is a poor fit for the first product? 6. What problem did you first misunderstand? 7. Who uses it without being reminded? 8. Why did the most recent user stop? 9. Who approves the purchase? 10. What makes switching hard?

**Acquisition, usage and economics.** 11. Which acquisition path has actually produced users? 12. How do customers outside your personal network differ? 13. What does "active" mean here? 14. What changes if you remove staff, test accounts and duplicates? 15. Which cohort returns, and when? 16. What exactly counts as revenue? 17. How much work is still manual? 18. What happens to costs on a hard task? 19. Is recent growth recurring or a one-time event? 20. What hasn't been observed long enough yet?

**Technical ownership and advantage.** 21. Who can fix the core product when it breaks? 22. Which dependency could stop delivery? 23. What's the most important failure mode? 24. How do you know the result is correct? 25. Where does the AI model hand control back to software or a person? 26. Which performance claim has the weakest test? 27. Why can't an existing alternative serve this first customer well? 28. Where is the alternative better? 29. Why is now the time? 30. What advantage gets stronger with use?

**Market, founders and next decisions.** 31. What buyer count does your market estimate use? 32. What has to happen to expand past the first task? 33. What relevant thing has each founder personally done? 34. What have you built together? 35. How do you decide when you disagree? 36. What could keep a founder from committing? 37. What changed since the last application or interview? 38. What result would make you reconsider the idea? 39. What's the biggest thing you still don't know? 40. What useful work happens next regardless of YC's decision?

## Prep tips from accepted founders

REPORTED. Tim Cherkasov (Trace, S25) suggests answers of 15 seconds or less, letting partners pull you deeper. Anna Sun (Nowadays, S23) split questions roughly by domain between founders and says not to pile on to each other's answers. Taranjeet Singh opened his successful interview with a 60-second frame covering product, problem, who it's for, traction and how big it can get. One team lost about a minute of their ten to a camera failure, so test the setup. Mock interviews with alumni or investors help find holes; take their feedback with a grain of salt and don't memorize.

## Mock interview protocol (for the coach)

1. Set the scene: "You're on Zoom with three YC partners. Ten minutes. Starting now." Play the partners; you can give them names like Partner A, B and C.
2. Open with "So what are you working on?"
3. One question at a time. Keep the pace up. If an answer runs long, interrupt politely ("got it, how many of those pay you?").
4. Steer toward the application's weakest link and stay on it for several questions, the way a partner would. Ask about anything in the written answers that looked vague or inflated.
5. Mix standard questions with company-specific ones. Ask at least one hard question from the list above.
6. Don't coach during the mock. Stay in character until the founder has given answers covering roughly 10 minutes (about 15 to 30 questions in a text simulation), or until the founder says stop.
7. Then debrief:
   - The story a partner would now tell about the company, in three sentences.
   - Answers that ran long, dodged the question, or opened with background instead of the answer.
   - Numbers they didn't know or that contradicted the application.
   - Where they sounded defensive or vague.
   - The three answers to rework, with a tighter version of each in the founder's own words.
   - Whether the weakest link is now addressed or still open.
8. Offer a second round focused on the weak areas.

Scoring for the debrief, 1 to 5 each: clarity (point first, plain words), command of numbers, honesty about weak spots, conciseness, team balance (if multiple founders). No predicted outcome.

## After the interview

- Send only what was requested or a material correction.
- If rejected, read the stated concern literally. One short reply with new evidence that addresses that exact concern is reasonable; otherwise use it as the brief for the next application. See `reapply-update-rejection.md`.
- Silence for a while after an interview isn't always a no; some founders got calls days or weeks later.
