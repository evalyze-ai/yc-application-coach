# Evidence and metrics

Use this when drafting progress, users, revenue and idea answers, and when deciding what evidence a founder should go get before submitting. It's a preparation method, not YC's scoring system.

## Metric dictionary

Settle the definition before a number goes in the application. If no founder can explain how a number was produced, it isn't ready.

| Metric | Define before using |
|---|---|
| Registered | Accounts created, with tests and duplicates excluded |
| Activated | The first value-producing action, named |
| Active | A named action within a stated window (daily, weekly, monthly) |
| Customer | A paying account, or say explicitly what else you mean |
| Revenue | Collected, invoiced or recognized; the period; the currency |
| MRR | Recurring monthly revenue under one consistent method |
| ARR / run rate | Say it's annualized and from which month |
| Retention | Starting cohort, what counts as returning, the interval |
| Churn | Customer or revenue churn, and the period |
| Conversion | Numerator, denominator, window |
| Pipeline | Sales stage. Never revenue |
| Growth | Start and end values with dates, same definition throughout |

## Formulas that survive a follow-up question

General measurement tools, not YC thresholds. Pick one definition and use it everywhere.

| Measure | Calculation | Watch for |
|---|---|---|
| Period growth | (end - start) / start | Undefined from zero; give the two absolute values instead |
| Weekly or monthly growth rate | (end / start) ^ (1 / periods) - 1 | Say how many periods and which dates |
| Activation | users completing the first-value action / eligible new users | Logging in or getting an invite usually isn't value |
| Cohort retention | cohort users meeting the return condition / original cohort | State the window and what "return" means |
| Customer retention | starting customers still qualifying at the end / starting customers | New customers don't belong in the top number |
| Net revenue retention | (starting recurring revenue + expansion - contraction - churn) / starting recurring revenue | Excludes new customers; tiny cohorts swing hard |
| Gross margin | (revenue - direct delivery cost) / revenue | For AI or service businesses, include human review and model cost |
| Acquisition cost | acquisition spend / customers acquired in the same period | Founder-led sales don't estimate a paid channel |
| Take rate | revenue / transaction volume | GMV isn't revenue; handle refunds consistently |
| AI task completion | successful completions / attempted tasks | Success means the job got done, not that the model replied |

A metric note a founder can keep beside the camera: Metric, as of date, who's included, the qualifying action or payment, the period, the value, the source (billing, analytics), exclusions (staff, tests, duplicates, refunds), and the known limit (small sample, short history).

## Commercial states: keep them separate

| State | Shows | Doesn't show |
|---|---|---|
| Verbal interest | Someone says they might want it | Budget, authority, a purchase or use |
| Nonbinding LOI | Specific conditional interest | A sale, cash or recurring revenue |
| Pilot | Someone is trying it in a defined setting | Payment or conversion, unless shown |
| Signed agreement | Terms exist, with their conditions | Cash received or successful delivery |
| Paid invoice | Money arrived for specified work | Renewal, repeat use or a repeatable channel |
| Production use | The product does real work | Profitable delivery or durable retention |

In the application, name the state exactly: "2 signed pilots, $4K collected, 1 converted to a $1.5K monthly contract in September" beats "3 enterprise customers." If customers pay for software and manual work together, separate them as far as the records allow and say what people do by hand.

## Dot plots: see whether individual users keep getting value

YC's David Lieb (July 2026) shows a simple chart: one row per user, one column per week (or whatever matches how often the product is naturally used), a dot when that user did the action that delivers value. Logins can look great while hiding weak usefulness, and growing active-user totals can hide new users replacing ones who left. His B2B example separates ten purchased seats from three activated users with weak use afterward; a signed annual contract didn't prevent churn. Before writing "are people using your product," a founder can build one from their analytics export in an hour. Know both the commercial number (seats, contract value) and the usage behind it, and report a small sample honestly.

## Evidence ladder

Higher rungs cost the customer more, so they tell a partner more. This is a thinking tool, not YC's ranking, and the right evidence depends on the business.

| Evidence | Can support | Doesn't establish |
|---|---|---|
| Founder's own frustration | Motivation | That others share it |
| User interviews | Understanding of the problem | That anyone will adopt |
| Waitlist or signups | Interest | Usage |
| Letter of intent | Stated intent | Revenue |
| Pilot | Willingness to test | Lasting adoption |
| Repeat use | Ongoing value | Good economics |
| Payment | Willingness to pay | Retention |
| A customer who depends on it | Real value even when imperfect | Broad demand |
| Renewal, expansion | Continuing value | A large market |

Daivik Goel (Shor, S25) says his accepted application was carried by a customer who refused to let them take down a buggy two-week-old prototype, and argues customer dependency matters more than revenue totals. VERIFIED (founder's guide). JC, a YC alum, puts the distinction well: building and talking to people are actions; users, revenue, signed letters of intent and real waitlists are traction. Lead with traction when you have it.

## Show the lever behind the line

Revenue that grew because a founder made a decision (a pricing change, a new channel, a narrower segment) is stronger evidence than revenue that grew because time passed. When describing growth, name what caused it.

## Small numbers are fine, presented honestly

- Show absolute values and dates next to any percentage. "300% growth" from 3 to 12 users is accurate and uninformative alone.
- Two or three dated points let a reader see the slope. A single number shows nothing about speed.
- Mention even tiny wins: a couple of real users, $5 of revenue, one pilot. Founders undersell more often than they oversell.

## Common distortions to catch

- GMV or transaction volume called revenue
- Services revenue blended into software revenue
- One-time setup fees annualized
- Free trials counted as customers
- GitHub stars counted as users
- Signed contracts counted as cash
- Cumulative users compared with monthly active users
- Pipeline or LOIs in the revenue answer
- Prior company or prior product traction presented as this company's
- Post-batch growth presented as application-time traction (when studying examples)

## What to lead with, by stage

YC funds every stage. The progress answers have one job: show speed at whatever stage you're in.

| Stage | Lead with | What partners check |
|---|---|---|
| Idea only | Why you, how many users you've talked to, the sharpest thing you learned, what ships in the next few weeks | Whether this team builds fast and understands the user |
| Prototype | A working demo, how fast you built it, user reactions in their words, what failed | Speed and technical skill |
| Launched, no revenue | Weekly active users, retention, where users come from, two or three dated points, why you aren't charging yet and the pricing test | Real pull, and whether you know your numbers |
| Revenue | Monthly revenue by month, paying customers, the biggest customer and what they pay, growth rate, concentration | Momentum and the slope |
| Hard tech / bio | Technical milestones (what works, tested how, by whom), the first pilot or customer, the next de-risking step, what the money buys | Feasibility and the team's depth |

## What to show, by business model

| Model | Evidence to emphasize | Common distortion |
|---|---|---|
| B2B software | Buyer, activation, deployment, retention, price, expansion | Pipeline presented as revenue |
| Developer tools / open source | Time to value, active developers, production use, paid conversion | Stars presented as users |
| Consumer | Activation, natural usage frequency, retention cohorts, acquisition cost | Downloads presented as engagement |
| Marketplace | Liquidity, repeat transactions on both sides, take rate, net revenue | GMV presented as revenue |
| AI-enabled service | Delivery, margin, human labor per account, what's automated today | Manual work described as automation |
| Hardware | Working prototype, cost per unit, manufacturing path, pilot | Prototype called production-ready |
| Regulated | Approval path, who is responsible, data controls | Planned compliance called approval |

## AI products: explain the work, not the model

For any product where AI does the work, the application should make these clear, briefly:
- The job completed, for whom, and what output they get.
- What the model does, what ordinary code does, where a human reviews.
- How quality is measured (real tasks, error rates, comparison with the customer's old process).
- What happens when the model is wrong.
- Cost per successful task, if relevant to the business model.

A model API doesn't make a product unfundable, and "we use AI" doesn't make it defensible. Explain workflow depth, reliability, distribution or data access only where they're real.

## Customer discovery that improves answers

If the idea or competitor answers are weak, the fastest fix is usually five to ten real conversations, asked about the last time the problem happened rather than "would you use this":
- What happened last time? Who was involved? What tools did they use?
- What did it cost in time or money? What did they try before?
- Who can approve a purchase? What would stop them switching?
- What happens if nothing changes?

Then write down one decision that changed because of what you heard. "We talked to 40 users" is weaker than "40 conversations showed the office manager, not the owner, picks the tool, so we rebuilt onboarding for her."

## Market size, built from the bottom

- Start with the first customer segment you can name, how many exist (with a source), what they'd pay (with evidence or a labeled assumption), and how you reach them.
- Then the expansion path: adjacent customers, workflows or geographies, and why you can reach them from where you start.
- Label which inputs are tested and which are assumptions.
- Avoid a huge industry total times an arbitrary share.
- If you can't source the customer count, write the math per thousand ("every 1,000 clinics at $400 a month is $4.8M a year") and add `[NEED: count of X, with source]` for the founder to fill. That keeps the reasoning visible without inventing a number.
- For automation, robotics and services, price against what the product replaces (driver hours, staff time, outsourced fees). A low one-time price times a modest customer count is often why partners say a market looks small; value-based pricing usually changes both the price and the story.
