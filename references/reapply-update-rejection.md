# Reapplying, updates and rejections

## Facts to anchor on

- YC's FAQ says about half of the companies in a typical batch applied more than once before being accepted, and strongly encourages reapplying after progress. For Winter 2024, YC's Hacker News moderator Daniel Gackle (dang) reported 58% of funded companies had applied before, with most having applied 0 to 3 times previously and the most being 6. VERIFIED.
- You must fill out a new application each time, and can reference past ones from your account page. Recommendations carry over. VERIFIED (FAQ).
- One application per batch. VERIFIED (FAQ).
- Feedback goes only to teams invited to interview. VERIFIED (FAQ).
- YC's "why not" page explains that a no without an interview usually has no specific reason: the median application is pretty good and capacity is fixed. VERIFIED.

## Reapplication plan

1. **Read the rejection.** After an interview, the email names the main concern. Without one, assume nothing stood out enough, and ask a YC alum or sharp outsider to name the weakest answer.
2. **Decide what would change a partner's mind.** Usually new evidence (customers, revenue, a working product), a stronger team (a technical cofounder, everyone full-time), or a better idea. Better wording alone rarely does it.
3. **Set a trigger.** A revenue number, a usage number, a launch, a hire. Reapply when it's hit, so you never reapply with nothing new.
4. **Write the "what changed" answer first,** with a before-and-after in comparable numbers and the decision behind the change.
5. **Rewrite the rest fresh.** Don't edit the old application line by line. Partners may compare.

**Apply now or wait for the trigger?** With four batches a year the next deadline is close, so founders often ask this right after a no. If there's measurable change on the concern that sank the last application, apply now. If there isn't, use the days before the deadline to get some; if that's impossible, applying anyway costs little, but expect it to read the same, and set the trigger for the batch after.

## The "what changed" answer

> Last application ([batch]): [metric A], [metric B], [status].
> Now: [metric A], [metric B], [status].
> What changed: we [decision] because [what we learned], which led to [result].
> Still unproven: [the remaining risk], which we're testing by [test].

Keep definitions identical between before and after, or say what changed.

## Updates after submitting

YC's FAQ says applications can't be edited continuously; material changes go through an update form (its example: adding or removing a cofounder). Founders have also used updates for a first paying customer or a revenue jump.

Send one only when it changes the picture: first paying customer, a launch that worked, a revenue jump, a signed pilot, a cofounder joining or going full-time. One or two at most.

**Template (3 to 4 sentences)**
> Update as of [date]: [the change, with a number]. When we applied, [previous state]. This matters because [connects to a likely concern]. [Optional link.]

Also update every place in the application that the change touches, if the form allows. Metorial's founder noted their revenue answer still said no revenue after a first customer was added in an update.

## After an interview rejection

- Read the concern literally and compare it with what customers are telling you. Don't pivot only to satisfy one partner if customer behavior points elsewhere.
- One short reply is reasonable if, and only if, it comes within days of the decision and carries new evidence on that exact concern. Weeks later, the next application is the reply. Founder accounts include a team that replied within the hour with product usage data and a clearer explanation and got a second interview, and a team that built the "missing" feature over a weekend and didn't get a reversal. Never argue with the decision.

**Template**
> Thanks for the feedback on [concern]. Since the interview, [new fact with number]. [One sentence on why it addresses the concern.] Happy to share more if useful.

## Partners sometimes come back

Founders have reported partners reaching out after a rejection when the company made visible progress (InsForge was offered a spot by a partner after a strong launch week, months after an interview rejection). You can't plan for it, but keep shipping and keep your inbox open.
