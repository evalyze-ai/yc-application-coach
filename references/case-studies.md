# Real applications and founder stories

Use these to show a founder what a strong answer looks like, and to make a point concrete. Ground rules when citing them:
- A published accepted application shows what was submitted, not why it was accepted. Teams, timing and businesses differ.
- Accepted applications contain mistakes. Don't copy one because it got in.
- Don't backdate. A company's current website or YC profile describes it now, not at application time.
- Some websites publish "real YC applications" that are reconstructions or contain fabricated answers. Only trust text that traces to the founder's own page, YC's site, or a spot-checked aggregator.

Labels: VERIFIED means the source was opened in October 2026 research. LIKELY means a founder-published source quoted by research guides that wasn't re-opened directly.

## Recent before-and-after cases

### Metorial: rejected Summer 2025 after interview, accepted Fall 2025 (VERIFIED)
Source: the founders published both applications (metorial.com/blog/open-sourcing-the-yc-application, Aug 17, 2026).
- **Why the first was rejected:** partners questioned how Metorial would stand out among many MCP projects and become a defensible business.
- **What changed:** the one-liner moved from "AI integrations in a single line of code" to "The MCP integration platform for agentic AI." The product widened into a fuller integration platform. Progress went from no users to 350 developers signed up for early access, GitHub stars from about 700 to about 2,600, and an enterprise pilot with a pharma company's developer team. Both applications reported no revenue at submission; a first paying customer came while the second was still editable.
- **The founder's own critique:** the first pitch supplied the very frame used against it; the product answer buried what it was under implementation detail; the market estimate started from a big developer population and assumed a share; they forgot to update the revenue answer after the first buyer; the second application described what they'd built in response to the criticism instead of insisting the moat was strong.
- **Lesson:** fix the concern with work, then describe what exists plainly. Keep every answer consistent after an update.

### Shor (Daivik Goel): accepted Summer 2025 on the fourth application (VERIFIED)
Source: shorpay.com/blog/yc-application-guide.
- First three applications rejected without interview. The fourth was started an hour before the deadline.
- What carried it: a customer who depended on a two-week-old prototype so much they wouldn't let the team take it down, bugs and all. His line: customer dependency beats ARR.
- His advice: stop engineering the perfect application, explain the business simply, be yourself; in the interview keep answers short and let partners follow up; don't bluff; be ready to explain why each founder has their role and how you resolve conflict; don't pick a cofounder to look good to YC.
- **Lesson:** substance written plainly beats polish. The fast application worked because the company already had something real to report.

### InsForge (Hang Huang): rejected after interview, then offered a spot, Spring 2026 (VERIFIED)
Source: insforge.dev/blog/getting-into-yc-after-6-tries.
- Six applications, six rejections. After the fifth reached interview, the partner cited revenue around $300 a month and no clear path to selling to enterprises. A later rejection cited a crowded category with strong incumbents and no proven moat.
- They ran 20 deep user interviews, found their real users were small AI-native teams, dropped the enterprise focus, and doubled down on the channel already working. Hosted databases grew from about 2,300 to over 4,000 in two months. A launch week drew a #1 spot on Product Hunt and GitHub Trending.
- A YC partner then asked to catch up and offered them a spot.
- **Lesson:** learn who your actual users are, focus there, and keep executing after a no. Don't read the launch metrics as the cause; they're part of one founder's account.

### Agent37: accepted Fall 2026 after 14 rejections (title VERIFIED, application details LIKELY)
Source: the founder's Hacker News post "My accepted YC application F26, after 14 rejections," linking the application PDF; the company launched on HN as a YC F26 startup in October 2026.
- Solo, bootstrapped founder. One-liner: "Persistent sandboxes for always-on AI agents."
- Progress led with numbers: over 1,150 paid instances, July revenue of $13.6K split into recurring and one-time pilots, $0 raised.
- Pivot story: earlier applications with a dashboard product; customers asked to embed agents in their own products and one paid for a pilot before the API existed. "My customers fixed the idea."
- Competitors: named the real incumbent (developers building it themselves on cloud servers), with a price comparison and the technical reason he could be cheaper.
- **Lesson:** every answer leads with a number or a concrete customer; a solo, bootstrapped founder never sounds defensive about either fact.

### Hyperspell: accepted Fall 2025 on the seventh application (LIKELY)
Source: a cofounder shared the written application and video publicly.
- Nearly every answer is a few short bullets, lowercase, unpolished. Revenue shown month by month with zeros left in. Competitors grouped by category with one line each. Two pivots summarized with the lesson learned.
- **Lesson:** clarity matters, polish doesn't.

### Clearly AI: accepted Summer 2024, pre-product (VERIFIED for the question list and one-liner)
Source: choigreene.com/guides/our-yc-application.
- One-liner: "Software security and privacy reviews using AI."
- Pre-product; progress reported customer conversations (reported as 46 prospective customers over 8 weeks), with part-time status stated.

**Other accounts with a specific lesson**

- **Ergo (W25):** the founder reports replacing a Zapier-based build with real software after partners questioned the team's coding ability. A lesson about showing the team can build, not a ban on no-code tools.
- **Captain (W26):** an earlier attempt had a broken demo and founders interrupting each other; the later one had a working demo and clearer answers. Test the demo and agree who answers what.
- **Lilac (S25):** reports getting in with an idea and no product, backed by relevant experience.
- **Scope (Spring 2026):** reports a first-try acceptance as a solo founder, with a narrowly described customer pain.
- **Cardboard (W26):** kept shipping while the application was under review; the founder says an update was followed by an interview the next day and acceptance the day after. That doesn't prove the update caused it.
- **SubImage (W25):** strong working relationship and security background, but trouble giving a crisp product explanation in the interview. Their first large contract closed near Demo Day after procurement delays, so it wasn't revenue at application time. Separate purchase intent, contracting and a signed deal.
- **PostHog (W20):** the founders' own account reports 400 signups, no revenue and two companies saying they'd pay at the time; payments came later, and they pivoted to today's product after the batch. Signups, promised purchases and collected cash are different facts.
- **Ascenix (F26, Toronto):** reports the founders lived in a retirement home to understand their users. Immersion is concrete evidence of understanding the customer.
- **LayerCI (2019 rejected, 2020 accepted, VERIFIED in the founders' public guide):** the rejected version had one founder, buzzwords and no launches. The accepted one had two full-time founders at 50/50, a demo video and website, about 1,000 conversations with potential users, some paying customers and public launches. Almost everything that changed was the company.

## Patterns across the stories

1. New evidence beats better writing. Most reapplicants who got in changed the company first.
2. Competition and market size are the most common stated reasons for rejection after interviews.
3. What you do after submitting can matter. Several founders got in partly because of progress after applying or after a rejection.
4. Fast applications that worked came from companies with substance already in hand.
5. Over-polishing hurts. Founders who compared their own versions say the winner was shorter and plainer.
