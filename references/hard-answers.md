# Hard answers: writing clearly when the facts are uncomfortable

Most founders have at least one fact they'd rather not write down. The pattern that works is the same every time: say the fact first, give the most relevant true context, show what you're doing about it, and stop. Partners respond better to a plain weak spot than to a defended one, and a hidden fact usually surfaces in the interview anyway.

These are response shapes, not reasons YC accepts or rejects anyone. Fill brackets only with facts the founder can support.

## 1. No customers yet

Say so first. Then the strongest real evidence and the specific behavior you're testing.

> We have no customers yet. Over the last [period] we talked to [N] [role], and [M] described [specific pain, their words]. [K] agreed to [concrete next step: trial, data access, weekly session]. We're testing whether they [behavior] with the prototype by [date].

Avoid upgrading testers, interview subjects or waitlist signups into users. If the evidence is thin, the fix is work, not a grander sentence.

## 2. No revenue

> No revenue. [Defined users] use [working scope] for [task], and [repeat behavior]. We haven't charged because [true reason]; we'll test [price] with [segment] by [date].

YC's FAQ says most funded companies have no revenue. A plain "no" plus usage evidence reads better than a pivot to the market size.

## 3. Revenue is small, flat, declining or concentrated

Show the monthly series as it is, including the bad month. Explain each change with what actually happened (a contract ended, a one-time setup fee, a price change, seasonality), and keep explanations separate from guesses.

> Monthly revenue (USD): Jun $1,200, Jul $3,100, Aug $2,400, Sep $2,600. July included a one-time $900 setup fee. One customer is [X]% of revenue; [what has and hasn't repeated with other buyers].

Name concentration when it matters. One big customer can be strong evidence and a real dependency at the same time. Never hide a weak recent month inside a cumulative total.

## 4. A founder is still employed or in school

> [Name] works at [employer] until [date] and goes full-time on [date]. [Name] has been full-time since [date].

Keep a conditional plan conditional ("will leave if accepted" is honest; "has resigned" when they haven't is not). Mention obligations that affect time or IP (a non-compete, a university IP policy, an employer whose customers overlap). Students who want to finish first can use Early Decision. Don't write a dramatic quit-my-job story.

## 5. Working on it a long time with modest progress

Partners read time worked against progress, so this needs an honest explanation tied to milestones, not a reset clock.

> We started exploring this in [month year], part-time. The current product started in [month year]; both founders have been full-time since [month year]. In the [N] months since, we [shipped X, got Y].

If a long research or build phase was necessary, explain the milestones it produced. If it wasn't, say what changed in how you work now. Never reset the start date after every pivot to look faster.

## 6. The founders met recently

> We met in [month year] through [context]. Since then we've built [project] together, which [result]. [How you divide work and decide].

A short history with proof of shipping together beats a long friendship with nothing built. If you haven't met in person, say so and when you will.

## 7. A cofounder left, or the team changed

State the current team and the practical effect: who owns the code now, how the equity was handled, what changed in progress. Separate what's agreed from what's still being settled. Use the update form if it happens after submitting.

> [Name] left in [month] and [vested/returned] their equity. [Name] now owns [area]. Since then we've [progress].

Avoid turning the application into a dispute, adding a rushed new cofounder for optics, or leaving partners to discover it in the interview.

## 8. Solo founder

> No. I cover [building and selling] today: I built [X] and closed [Y]. [The gap I'm aware of] is covered by [plan or person], and I'd add a cofounder when [real condition].

One or two sentences, then move on. In the published record, the solo founders who got in stated it flatly; long defenses read badly. YC said in September 2026 that nearly one in five of its companies has a solo founder.

## 9. No technical founder, or an agency built it

This is the hardest case, because YC's FAQ says the team should be able to build the product. Be exact:

> [Agency] built the MVP between [month] and [month] under a [work-for-hire] agreement; the company owns the code and IP. [Founder] [manages / is learning / has shipped X in the codebase since]. We're looking for a technical cofounder and have [concrete step].

Get the facts in writing first: who owns the code and IP, whether the agency holds or is owed equity, any retainer, who fixes a production bug tomorrow. If nothing can honestly be said about in-house ability, say what you're doing about it and consider whether adding real technical talent before applying is the better move.

## 10. Important work is still manual

> Customers get [outcome]. Today the software does [steps]; [person] does [step] by hand for each [unit], which takes about [time]. We're automating [step] next, measured by [test].

Include the human cost in your economics where it matters. Label manual steps in the demo. Never edit a demo or write an answer that implies a service is already autonomous software.

## 11. Long development cycle (hard tech, bio, hardware)

Lead with completed technical milestones and the next one that matters, using the right words for what's been shown (simulation, bench result, prototype, independent test, pilot, production design).

> We've [measured result] on [bench / prototype] under [conditions]. The next milestone is [X] by [date], which proves [assumption]. [Who needs the eventual product and the evidence: LOI with conditions, pilot, paid study].

Don't import consumer-app metrics. A long cycle still needs an explanation of who needs the result. If "capital intensive" is the worry, answer with unit cost, margin, cash per deployment and whether customers prepay or lease.

## 12. The customer can't be named

> A [size] [industry] company with [relevant detail] has used it since [date] for [task], paying $[X]/month. (Name confidential under NDA.)

Keep enough specificity to make the evidence real. Know what proof you could share if asked (redacted invoice, a reference call). Never imply a contract with a famous logo when it's a conversation.

## 13. The product is built on a common model or API

> We use [model] for [step]. What we build is [workflow, integrations, evaluation, reliability work]. On [real task], we [measured result] versus [baseline]. If models get better at [X], [honest view of what that does to us].

A model API doesn't disqualify a product, and "we use AI" isn't a moat. Don't invent proprietary data or a defensibility story you can't support; say what's true now and what you think could compound.

## 14. A YC company (or a big company) already does this

YC's FAQ says funding a similar company isn't a factor. Answer the real question, which is why customers would choose you:

> [Competitor] is built for [user/use]. Our first customers are [segment], who need [specific thing]; [evidence: who switched or chose you and why]. Where [competitor] is better: [honest point].

If partners are likely to say "we've seen this before," this answer has to carry the difference. Founders who share post-interview rejections most often cite unclear differentiation.

## 15. A previous company failed

> I previously founded [company] ([what it did], [what happened]). We learned [specific lesson], which is why we [specific decision now].

Name the hypothesis that failed and the behavior that changed. "We learned to persevere" says nothing. Don't erase the company, inflate an exit, or carry its revenue into this one.

## 16. Recently pivoted

> We started on [old idea] in [month]. [What happened, with numbers]. Talking to [users] showed [insight], so in [month] we switched to [new idea]. Since then: [dated evidence].

Keep old-product traction separate from new-product evidence. If you pivot after submitting and before an interview, send an update and bring evidence for the new direction.

## 17. Reapplying with little change

Be honest with the founder before writing anything: a reapplication with nothing new on the concern that sank the last one tends to get the same answer. Options, in order:
- get measurable change on that concern before the deadline (customers, a working product, a technical cofounder, full-time commitment);
- if the change is real but small, show it precisely in the before-and-after;
- if there's nothing, applying still costs little, but set a trigger for a later batch.

Never pretend the change is bigger than it is.

## 18. Already raised a lot, or did another accelerator

> We raised $[X] on a [SAFE / priced round] at a $[cap] cap from [investors] in [month]. Since then we've [progress].

YC's FAQ says many batch companies have raised over $1M and prior higher valuations aren't a barrier. With a previous accelerator, YC may expect more progress, so show what happened since.

## 19. Lopsided equity or an unusual structure

Give the split and one sentence of reason that holds up ("[Name] joined [N] months after launch with a smaller stake that vests from [date]"). If the reason is "it was my idea" or "I started earlier," reconsider the split on its merits, because partners have said those reasons don't hold. Explain odd structures (a holding company, a studio, a non-US entity) plainly; YC helps companies outside the US, Canada, Cayman and Singapore create a parent company after acceptance.

## 20. The demo is rough or partly simulated

Include it anyway, and label what's real. "This runs locally on sample data; [step] is done by hand today" is fine. YC's own demo tip says unpolished is fine. A staged or edited demo presented as real is not.

## 21. Personal circumstances that affect availability

Health, family, visas, notice periods: say only what affects the company, briefly, with dates if relevant ("[Name] finishes a notice period on [date]"). Partners need to know availability, not personal detail. Don't overshare drama that doesn't answer the question.
